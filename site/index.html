NYC taxes million-dollar penthouses at a lower rate than modest family homes.

Because co-ops and condos are legally assessed as if they were rental buildings — not on what they actually sold for — ultra-luxury condos often pay a smaller share of their real value in tax than a two-family home in the outer boroughs. Scroll to see it, building by building.

Take Ken Griffin’s penthouse.

220 Central Park South, unit 50. Sold January 23, 2019 for $239,958,219 — at the time, the most expensive home ever sold in the U.S.

The city’s own FY2026 assessment values it at just $6,725,023. At the class-2 rate (12.439%), that computes to about $836,500 in tax — an effective rate of roughly 0.35% of what it actually sold for.

It’s not just one building.

Across thousands of sale-verified properties citywide, effective rates fall as price rises for both groups — but not for the same reason. Co-op/condo buildings (green) are undervalued by the income-approach method above. 1–3 family homes (blue) are assessed closer to true value in principle, but New York State caps how fast assessed value can rise each year — so the priciest, fastest-appreciating homes end up under-assessed too.

The dashed lines are each group’s trend, fit to this data — not eyeballed. Both tilt down; in this sample, 1–3 family’s decline is at least as steep.
